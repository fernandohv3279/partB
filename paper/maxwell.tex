Maxwell's equations govern the behavior of electromagnetic fields and form the
foundation of classical electromagnetism. These equations describe how electric
and magnetic fields interact and propagate through space. In their differential
form, they read
\begin{align*}
\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{E} &= \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\\
\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{B} &= 0\\
\nabla \times \boldsymbol{E} &= -\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{B}}{\partial t}\\
\nabla \times \boldsymbol{B} &=
\frac{1}{c^2}\cdot\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}{\partial t}
+ \mu_0 \boldsymbol{J}.
\end{align*}
We refer to this form as the microscopic form of Maxwell's equations.
Another formulation, called the macroscopic form, better suits our purposes when
working with material parameters. In vacuum, which is our focus in this paper,
the constitutive relations are
\(\varepsilon_0\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{D}\),
\(\boldsymbol{B}=\mu_0\boldsymbol{H}\), and
\(\mu_0\varepsilon_0=\frac{1}{c^2}\). These lead to:
\begin{align*}
\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{D} &= \rho\\
\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{B} &= 0\\
\nabla \times \boldsymbol{E} &= -\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{B}}{\partial t}\\
\nabla \times \boldsymbol{H} &=  \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{D}}{\partial t}
+ \boldsymbol{J}.
\end{align*}
For dielectric materials, the constants \(\varepsilon_0\)
and \(\mu_0\) generalize to matrices, and the constitutive relations become
\(\varepsilon^{ij}E_j=D_i\) and \(B_i=\mu^{ij}H_j\) (Einstein notation).

Maxwell's equations involve two key differential
operators: the divergence and the curl. Unlike the scalar Helmholtz
equation, electromagnetic fields are
vector quantities, and their transformation under coordinate
changes involves both operators.
Understanding how these operators transform is essential for
deriving the material parameters that achieve cloaking.

\subsection{Form invariance of Maxwell's equations}

Maxwell's equations possess a remarkable property called form invariance: they
maintain their structure under any coordinate transformation. This property is
so fundamental that, according to \cite{mccallRoadmapTransformationOptics2018},
it served as one of the guiding principles Einstein used to develop general
relativity. This property also provides the key mathematical principle that
enables transformation optics to work with the full electromagnetic equations.

\subsection{The transformation}

We now construct a coordinate transformation that
creates a region of space where we can conceal objects. This region
must be decoupled from the original space. What does this
mean for the transformation? The transformation must map
a point, a line, or a small area into a larger area, which becomes our
cloaking region. Light will perceive this region as very small despite its
actual size. There are multiple choices for such transformation, in this paper,
we will use \eqref{transformation}, which is given in cylindrical coordinates
as follows:

For \(r'\leq b\)
\begin{equation}\label{transformation}
\begin{cases}
	r= \frac{b-a}{b} r' + a\\
	\theta = \theta'\\
	z=z'
\end{cases}.
\end{equation}

For \(r'\geq b\) the transformation is the identity. The primed coordinates
describe the virtual space, which represents the space that light perceives.
The unprimed coordinates describe the physical space---the actual space. Notice
that \(0\leq r'\leq b\) maps to \(a\leq r\leq b\), so the transformation maps
nothing to \(0\leq r \leq a\). This region will be cloaked, since from the
perspective of light it does not exist; or rather, light perceives it as a
single point (the origin).

\subsection{Encoding the transformation into the material parameters}

At this point, we could in principle follow the procedure outlined in Section
\ref{helmholtzexample}, using the chain rule to rewrite the operators in the
equations and derive the material parameters. However, this approach leads
to complicated and lengthy expressions that can be difficult to handle. The
transformation optics approach instead develops a general formula that
allows us to compute the material parameters for any transformation. We can
then substitute the specific transformation we want and obtain the parameters
directly from the formula. This approach requires more sophisticated tools from
differential geometry, but it simplifies computations and it is more elegant.
The aforementioned formula is:
\begin{equation}\label{formula}
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{\mu}=
\frac{\sqrt{g'}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
\left(\boldsymbol{g}'\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^T}{\det\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}.
\end{equation}
