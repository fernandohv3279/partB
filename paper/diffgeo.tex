In this section we introduce the basic tools that we need to derive formula
\eqref{formula}. Almost all the material from this section was taken from
section 3 of \cite{leonhardtGeometryLightScience2010}.

Let us remember that the main idea of cloaking is to observe the form that a
differential operator takes under a particular coordinate system and then
rewrite the equation, taking it back to it's original form. This is where
differential geometry becomes very useful, since it provides a way to write the
operators divergence and curl in arbitrary coordinates. This is what we will do
in the next sections.

\subsection{Divergence and curl}

In Cartesian coordinates and using Einstein notation the divergence operator is
described with the formula
\begin{equation*}
\nabla\cdot \boldsymbol{V} = \partial_i V^i.
\end{equation*}

We can write this in a coordinate free way. For a general coordinate system
with metric tensor \(g\), the divergence operator takes the form
\begin{equation}\label{divergence}
	\nabla\cdot \boldsymbol{V} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_i
	\left(\sqrt{g} V^i\right).
\end{equation}

Similarly for the curl we have the expression:
\begin{equation}\label{curl}
\left(\nabla\times \boldsymbol{V}\right)^i =\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_jV_k,
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon^{ijk}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}[ijk]\) is the Levi-Civita tensor.

Even though \eqref{divergence} and \eqref{curl} are very compact and simple
expressions, we have to be careful to interpret them correctly. The first
observation is that these formulas are in terms of a basis that has not been
normalized. Secondly, we must pay attention to the position of the indices,
these formulas make a distinction between contravariant (regular) vectors and
covariant vectors, next subsection will explain a bit more about this.

\subsection{Rising/Lowering indices}

In order to properly use the new formulas we need to learn how to lower or rise
indices, since the formulas require us to use (contravariant) vectors when we
have upper indices and covariant vectors when we have lower indices. The way we
do this is by means of the metric tensor (upper indices) and its inverse (lower
indices), as follows:
\begin{align*}
	V_i&=g_{ij}V^j\\
	g^{ki}V_i&=V^k.
\end{align*}

In the first equation we lower an index by multiplying by the inverse of the
metric tensor (namely \(g_{ij}\)) and in the second equation we rise an index
by multiplying by the metric tensor (namely \(g^{ki}\)).

Strictly speaking, vectors and covectors are different mathematical objects
that live in different spaces but for our purposes we can treat them as just
matrices and vectors. The above rules are all we need to apply the formulas.

\subsection{Relationships between metric tensors}
The last tool we need from differential geometry is an equation that relates
the metric tensors of two different coordinate systems.

If we have \{\(x^i\)\} and \{\(x^{i'}\)\} two different coordinate systems then
we define
\[
	\Lambda=\Lambda^i_{i'}=\left[\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial
	x^{i'}}\right],
\]
and the relationship between the tensors associated to each of the coordinate
systems is given by
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{g'}=\Lambda^T \boldsymbol{g} \Lambda.
\end{equation}
This equation will be used in the next section.
