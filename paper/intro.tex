Transformation optics employs coordinate transformations to design materials with
various interesting electromagnetic properties. Perhaps the most famous
application is invisibility cloaking. In 2006, Pendry et al. described the
parameters necessary to construct an invisibility cloak
\cite{pendryControllingElectromagneticFields2006}. They
expressed this description through the matrix:
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
=\boldsymbol{\mu}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{r-a}{r} & 0 & 0\\
0 & \frac{r}{r-a} & 0\\
0 & 0 & \left(\frac{b}{b-a}\right)^2 \frac{r-a}{r}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
What does this matrix represent? Why does it produce invisibility? What
assumptions underlie this result? Under what circumstances does it work?

This document aims to formulate these questions more rigorously, answer
them, and provide intuition for understanding the results.

\subsection{Structure of the paper}
We organize this paper as follows:

Section \ref{cloaking} provides a
general description of cloaking and explains how coordinate transformations can
manipulate light.

Section \ref{helmholtzexample} illustrates the main concept of
transformation optics through a simple example using the Helmholtz equation.

Section \ref{numerics} presents numerical experiments that validate the
theory.

Section \ref{maxwell} briefly explains Maxwell's equations and discusses the
challenges of applying transformation optics to these PDEs.

Section \ref{geodiff} introduces differential geometry tools that enable us to
handle coordinate transformations of Maxwell's equations.

Section \ref{cloakformula} synthesizes these concepts and derives
the formula for an invisibility cloak.
