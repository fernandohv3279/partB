The objective of cloaking is to guide electromagnetic waves around
a region of space in a way that the resulting wave, when measured
outside of this region is indistinguishable from a wave that
propagates through empty space. A famous example of this desired behaviour can
be seen on figure \ref{cloakImage}.
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/cylindricalcloak}
\caption{Cloaking device. The light bends around the inner circle
and comes out of the other end in the same way it would if there
was empty space instead of the cloaking device. Image taken from
\cite{pendryControllingElectromagneticFields2006}.}
\label{cloakImage}
\end{figure}

Since we want to bend light, the next question is: How can we do
so? There are multiple ways to achieve this but perhaps the most familiar
example would when light changes direction as it moves between two mediums that
have different refraction indices. This is commonly known as Snell's law; see
figure \ref{snell}. This is the simplest way of bending light, changing the
refraction index of the medium. This creates the question: Is it possible to
tweak the refraction index in a way that light bends in the very specific way
we want, namely in the way that figure \ref{cloakImage} shows?

An equivalent explanation of this phenomenon that is better for our purpose is
called Fermat's principle. To understand it better we need to first talk about
the refraction index of a medium; this is defined as the speed of light in that
medium divided by the speed of light in vacuum. So we can think of it as a
resistance felt by light. Fermat's principle says that light wants to go from
point A to point B as fast as possible; see Figure \ref{fermat}. We can recover
Snell's law from Fermat's principle and vice versa, but there is a nice
interpretation that we can give to Fermat's principle that will be useful to us
later. Such interpretation is the following: \textbf{modifying the composition
of the medium is equivalent to modifying the geometry that light perceives}.
For light, the relevant ``metric" is time, not distance. Both of these are
related of course, but in this geometry that light perceives the ``best" path
to go from A to B is no longer the obvious straight line, which minimizes
distance; instead is given precisely by Snell's law, which is the path that
minimizes time.
