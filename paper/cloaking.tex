The objective of cloaking is to guide electromagnetic waves around
a region of space such that the resulting wave, when measured
outside this region, remains indistinguishable from a wave that
propagates through empty space. Figure \ref{cloakImage} illustrates this desired behavior.

\end{figure}

Since we want to bend light, the natural question becomes: How can we
accomplish this? Multiple approaches exist, but perhaps the most familiar
example occurs when light changes direction as it moves between two media with
different refractive indices. We commonly know this phenomenon as Snell's law;
see Figure \ref{snell}. Changing the refractive index of the medium provides
the simplest way to bend light. This observation raises a crucial question: Can
we design a refractive index profile that bends light in the specific way
shown in Figure \ref{cloakImage}?

Fermat's principle offers an equivalent explanation of this phenomenon that
better suits our purposes. To understand it, we must first discuss the
refractive index of a medium. We define the refractive index as the ratio of
the speed of light in vacuum to the speed of light in that medium. We can
therefore interpret it as a resistance that light experiences. Fermat's
principle states that light travels from point A to point B along the path that
minimizes travel time; see Figure \ref{fermat}. We can derive Snell's law from
Fermat's principle and vice versa, but Fermat's principle admits a powerful
interpretation that will prove useful later. This interpretation is the
following: \textbf{modifying the composition of the medium is equivalent to
modifying the geometry that light perceives}. For light, the relevant
``metric'' measures time, not distance. These quantities are related, of
course, but in the geometry that light perceives, the ``best'' path from A to B
no longer follows the obvious straight line that minimizes distance. Instead,
Snell's law gives this optimal path, which minimizes time.
