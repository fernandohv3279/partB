\subsection{Deriving the formula for \(\varepsilon^{ij}\) and \(\mu^{ij}\)}
Using the tools from the previous section we can write Maxwell's equations in a
coordinate free way.
Then
\begin{align*}
\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{E} &= \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\\
\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{H} &= 0\\
\nabla\times \boldsymbol{E} &=-\mu_0\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{H}}{\partial t}\\
\nabla\times \boldsymbol{H} &=\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{J}
\end{align*}
becomes
\begin{align}
\begin{split}\label{maxwellGeneral}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_i\left(\sqrt{g}E^i\right) &= \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_i\left(\sqrt{g}H^i\right) &=0 \\
\epsilon^{ijk} \partial_jE_k &= -\mu_0\frac{\partial H^i}{\partial t}\\
\epsilon^{ijk} \partial_jH_k &= \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial E^i}{\partial t} + J^i,
\end{split}
\end{align}
lowering the indices on \(H^i,E^i\) and writing the Levi-Civita tensor explicitly as
\(\epsilon^{ijk}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}[ijk]\) we get
\begin{align*}
\partial_i\left(\sqrt{g}g^{ij}E_j\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{g}\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\\
\partial_i\left(\sqrt{g}g^{ij}H_j\right) &=0\\
[ijk]\partial_jE_k &= -\mu_0\frac{\partial\left(\pm \sqrt{g}g^{ij}H_j\right)}{\partial t}\\
[ijk]\partial_jH_k &= \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial \left(\pm \sqrt{g}g^{ij}E_j\right)}{\partial t} \pm \sqrt{g}J^i.
\end{align*}
If we take the constitutive equations 
\begin{align}
D^i&=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon^{ij}E_j \nonumber\\
B^i&=\mu_0\mu^{ij}H_j \nonumber\\
\varepsilon^{ij}&=\mu^{ij}=\pm\sqrt{g}g^{ij}\label{cartesiantensors}
\end{align}
and define \(\varrho=\sqrt{g}\rho\), \(\mathcal{J}^i=\pm\sqrt{g}J^i\), we
obtain Maxwell's equation in the macroscopic form!
\begin{align*}
\partial_i D^i&=\varrho\\
\partial_i B^i&=0\\
[ijk]\partial_jE_k&=-\frac{\partial B^i}{\partial t}\\
[ijk]\partial_jH_k&=\frac{\partial D^i}{\partial t}+\mathcal{J}^i.
\end{align*}
It is worth taking a moment to summarize what we are doing here. We start with
Maxwell's equations in Cartesian, then rewrite them in arbitrary coordinates
and then we interpret them in the original Cartesian system. Then equation
\eqref{cartesiantensors} gives us the parameters that would induce the
transformation. The limitation with this is that then we need our
transformation to be described in Cartesian coordinates. So we can't apply
formula \eqref{cartesiantensors} to transformation \eqref{transformation}
because it is given in cylindrical. In order to have more flexibility we start
with an arbitrary coordinate system, move to a different arbitrary coordinate
system and reinterpret the equations in the original coordinates. So what we do
is take \eqref{maxwellGeneral} and reinterpret it in the coordinate system
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_i\left(\sqrt{\gamma}E^i\right) &= \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\partial_i\left(\sqrt{\gamma}H^i\right) &=0\\
[ijk] \partial_jE_k &= -\mu_0\frac{\partial \left(\sqrt{\gamma} H^i\right)}{\partial t}\\
[ijk] \partial_jH_k &= \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial \left(\sqrt{\gamma} E^i\right)}{\partial t} + \sqrt{\gamma}J^i,
\end{align*}
which yields the constitutive equations:
\begin{align}
	D^i&=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon^{ij}E_j\nonumber\\
	B^i&=\mu_0\mu^{ij}H_j\nonumber\\
	\varepsilon^{ij}&=\mu^{ij}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{g}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}g^{ij}\label{tensorformulawithg}.
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{tensorformulawithg} gives a recipe to compute the material
parameters starting in an arbitrary coordinate system and with an arbitrary
coordinate transformation; however, the inverse metric tensor that appears in
the formula makes it, impractical, since it is not immediately obvious what
this is. This tensor describes the geometry that light perceives, but we would
like to instead have a formula that uses the coordinate transformation and our
original metric tensor, i.e. the metric of the space we started with. For this
we use the relationship between the tensors that we saw in the previous
section; namely, equation \eqref{tensorrelations}
\begin{equation*}
	\boldsymbol{g'}=\Lambda^T \boldsymbol{g} \Lambda.
\end{equation*}
Taking determinant on both sides we get
\[g'=g\left(\det\Lambda\right)^2.\]
Taking inverse on both sides we get
\[
\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}=\Lambda(\boldsymbol{g}')^{-1}\Lambda^T.
\]
Then
\begin{align*}
	\mu^{ij}=\varepsilon^{ij}&=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\sqrt{g}g^{ij}\\
		&=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\frac{\sqrt{g'}}{|\det\Lambda|}\Lambda(\boldsymbol{g}')^{-1}\Lambda^T\\
		&=\frac{\sqrt{g'}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\frac{\Lambda(\boldsymbol{g}')^{-1}\Lambda^T}{\det\Lambda}.
\end{align*}
Now this formula is better because it is in terms of the coordinate
transformation \(\Lambda\) and \(\boldsymbol{g}'\) which is the metric tensor
of the coordinate system we started with; in our particular case, cylindrical.
